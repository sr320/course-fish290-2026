CORE CONSIDERATIONS FOR A SCIENTIFIC MANUSCRIPT
Synthesized from FISH 290 readings (Heard chs. 10-13, 18, 20; Mensh & Kording 2017;
Schimel "Writing Science") and lectures (Weeks 2-5).

WHOLE-PAPER FRAME
- One central contribution. A reader should be able to give a one-sentence summary
  a year later. The title is the ultimate refinement of that contribution.
- Context-Content-Conclusion (C-C-C). At every scale - paper, section, paragraph -
  set context first, deliver content in the middle, end with the conclusion to be
  remembered.
- The Introduction and Discussion are mirrors: every question raised at the front
  must be answered at the back; nothing major in the Discussion should be a
  surprise.

INTRODUCTION
- Three Swalesian moves: (1) define the research territory (broad context),
  (2) establish a niche (the gap, contradiction, or unanswered question),
  (3) occupy the niche (your approach, and ideally a one-line preview of the result).
- Hourglass shape: start broad enough for the target journal, narrow steadily to
  one explicit question. Avoid a generic literature review.
- The reader should leave the Introduction able to state the gap and the question
  in their own words.

METHODS
- Organize logically, not chronologically. Subheads should mirror Results subheads
  so a reader can navigate back and forth.
- The function of Methods is credibility, not exact reproducibility. Include a
  detail if it (a) shows you are qualified, (b) makes the approach plausible, or
  (c) lets the reader follow the investigative logic into Results.
- Address methodological limitations where the method is described - not at the
  end. Use "but, yes": acknowledge briefly, explain why the method still answers
  the question.

RESULTS
- Treat Methods/Results/Discussion as a firewall. Results report what was observed:
  what was measured, in which comparison, with what magnitude, and with what
  statistical support. Interpretation belongs in the Discussion.
- Open the Results with the main finding (or, for complex arguments, with a brief
  preview followed by build-up). A "main-result-first" power position is read by
  more people than any other sentence.
- Each results paragraph should answer one question - set up by its first sentence,
  resolved by its last.
- Numbers: winnow to those that carry the story. Report only meaningful precision
  (two sig figs for P-values; magnitudes rounded to what the measurement supports).
  Do not duplicate the same number across text, table, and figure.

DISCUSSION
- Four components: (1) interpret the results to answer the research question,
  (2) consider weaknesses honestly, (3) relate findings to prior literature and
  draw broader implications, (4) propose specific (not generic) future directions.
- Open with the answer, not a recap of the results. The first paragraph is a
  power position.
- Hedge where uncertainty is real ("our results suggest", "if correct") but do not
  hedge into vagueness. Limitations belong early in the Discussion, not at the end.
- A separate Conclusion section is rarely needed; if used, keep it 4-6 sentences
  and avoid restating the abstract.

FIGURES AND TABLES
- Choose the format that matches the data: text for 2-3 numbers, tables for exact
  values across many entries, figures for trends, comparisons, and relationships.
- Each figure/table must stand alone (self-contained legend) AND be integrated
  with the text (the text tells the reader what pattern to look for).
- Keep figures simple: <= 4 panels, <= 4 traces per panel, axes starting at zero
  where possible, identical scales when comparing panels, color used only when
  necessary. Use thick lines and large fonts so figures survive reduction.
- A figure title/caption should name the comparison being made, not just label the
  axes. Cite each figure in the order it appears.

PROCESS REMINDERS
- Allocate effort by readership: title > abstract > figures > intro/discussion >
  results > methods.
- Outline before drafting. Write one sentence per planned paragraph and check that
  every paragraph advances the central contribution.
- Brevity is a revision task: cut hedges, nominalizations, metadiscourse, padding,
  and redundancy on a dedicated pass after the draft is complete.
