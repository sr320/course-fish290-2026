# Feedback Rubric: Criteria and Section-to-Guide Mapping

This file gives the agent two things for every section of a student draft:

1. **What to look for** — substantive criteria drawn from `comprehensive_guide_to_writing_a_scientific_paper.md`. Feedback bullets should reflect these criteria, not generic writing advice.
2. **Where to cite in the guide** — the exact section number(s) of `comprehensive_guide_to_writing_a_scientific_paper.md` that back each bullet.

Both must inform every feedback bullet: criteria drive the substance, the guide sections ground the citation.

The single source of truth for all feedback is:

- `comprehensive_guide_to_writing_a_scientific_paper.md` (referred to below as "the guide").

Do not cite individual `lectures/` or `readings/` files. Cite the guide and the specific section within it.

## Whole-paper criteria

These apply at every scale — paper, section, paragraph — and should inform the overall assessment in `SUMMARY`:

- **One central contribution.** A reader should be able to give a one-sentence summary of the paper a year later. The title is the ultimate refinement of that contribution. (Guide §2, Principle 1; §4)
- **Write for a flesh-and-blood human who doesn't know the work.** Define terms, avoid pronoun-heavy prose, minimize loose threads. (Guide §2, Principle 2)
- **Context–Content–Conclusion (C-C-C) at every scale.** Set context first, deliver content in the middle, end with the conclusion to be remembered — for the paper, each section, and each paragraph. (Guide §2, Principle 3)
- **Optimize logical flow.** No zig-zag; use parallel form for parallel ideas; re-use the same noun for the same concept. (Guide §2, Principle 4)
- **Introduction–Discussion mirror.** Every question raised in the Introduction must be answered in the Discussion; nothing major in the Discussion should be a surprise. (Guide §6; §9)
- **Allocate effort by readership.** title > abstract > figures > intro/discussion > results > methods. (Guide §11, "Allocate time to what readers actually read")
- **Brevity is a revision task.** Cut hedges, padding, redundancy, and bloated phrases on a dedicated pass after the draft is complete. (Guide §12)

## By manuscript section

For every section below, "Guide refs" lists the section numbers (and named subsections) of `comprehensive_guide_to_writing_a_scientific_paper.md` that must be drawn upon when writing feedback for that section.

### Title

What to look for:

- States the central contribution, not just the topic.
- Calibrated to the target journal's audience; right scope (not too broad, not too narrow).
- Active and informative rather than coy.

Guide refs: §4 (Title); §2 (Principle 1).

### Abstract / summary paragraph

What to look for:

- Broad → narrow → broad C-C-C arc: orient the field, narrow to the gap, state approach + key result, end with broader meaning.
- Typical length 150–300 words.
- No in-text citations; no detailed statistics (sample sizes OK if easy to digest).
- The result "fits the gap like a key fits its lock" — the abstract delivers the result the gap demanded.

Guide refs: §5 (Abstract); §2 (Principle 3, C-C-C).

### Introduction

What to look for:

- Three Swalesian moves: (1) define the research territory, (2) establish a niche (the gap), (3) occupy the niche (approach + objectives).
- Progressive narrowing — broad at top, narrowing steadily to one explicit question. Not a generic literature review.
- Each Introduction paragraph (except the last) has a topic sentence, supporting middle, and a take-home sentence that lands on the relevant unknown.
- The final paragraph compactly states the objectives (quantitatively when applicable) and previews only briefly, if at all.
- A reader should leave able to state the gap and the question in their own words.

Guide refs: §6 (Introduction); §2 (Principles 1 and 3).

### Methods

What to look for:

- Three jobs: enable replication, provide context for the Results, let readers judge credibility.
- Organized logically with subheadings (not chronology). Subheads ideally mirror Results subheads so readers can move back and forth.
- Past tense; active voice when possible.
- Conventions: spell out abbreviations on first use; metric units; military time; numerals/spelled-out numbers rule; scientific names italicized; "fish" vs "fishes" used correctly.
- "Theory of mind" — no pronoun-heavy ambiguity; reader can build a clear mental picture of sites, designs, apparatus.

Guide refs: §7 (Methods); §2 (Principle 2).

### Results (text, figures, tables, statistics)

What to look for in the text:

- Firewall between Methods, Results, and Discussion. Results report what was observed — pattern + magnitude + statistical support. Biological interpretation belongs in the Discussion.
- First paragraph briefly summarizes the overall approach (most readers skip Methods).
- Each Results paragraph follows C-C-C: set up a question, present data and logic, end with a declarative sentence that answers it.
- Pair the pattern with the evidence in a single sentence — e.g., "Fertilized seeds were larger than unfertilized seeds (mean 1.8 vs. 0.9 g, p = 0.019; Fig. 1)." Avoid bare figure call-outs and bare numbers without interpretation of the pattern.
- Subheadings (where used) mirror the Methods.
- Past tense; just the facts.

What to look for in figures and tables:

- Right format for the data: sentence for 2–3 numbers; figure for trends/relationships; table for precise values across many entries.
- Figure titles state the conclusion of the analysis; captions explain how the data were obtained (axes, symbols, n, error bars, tests, bounds), without interpretation.
- Captions go below figures, above tables; figures and tables numbered in separate series in order of appearance; every figure/table cited in body text.
- Each figure/table stands alone with its caption.
- Tables: context columns on left, dependent values on right; consistent significant figures; horizontal lines above header and below last row only; bold significant p-values.

Guide refs: §8 (Results, including "Pair the pattern with the evidence," "Figures," "When to use a figure vs. a table vs. a sentence," "Making tables that aren't painful," "Figure/table mechanics").

### Discussion

What to look for:

- Three jobs: explain how the gap was filled, acknowledge limitations honestly, position the contribution for future work.
- Whole-section shape is the mirror of the Introduction: start specific (interpret your data against your objectives), end broad (back to the level you opened the Introduction with).
- Paragraph 1 answers the question in plain language without statistics — a pivotal paragraph.
- Each major finding gets its own interpretation paragraph: biological meaning, alternative explanations, agreement/disagreement with prior literature, generalizability.
- Limitations addressed honestly, not in the final paragraph. Prefer "But, yes…" framing over "Yes, but…".
- Concrete, specific future directions — never "more research is needed."
- Statistics belong in Results, not the Discussion.
- Citations belong in interpretation, limitations, and broader-implications paragraphs.

Guide refs: §9 (Discussion); §2 (Principle 3, C-C-C).

### Acknowledgments and References

What to look for:

- Acknowledgments name non-author contributors and disclose funding/grant numbers; kept professional.
- Every in-text citation appears in the reference list, and nothing else (no "bibliography of relevant papers").
- In-text citation form: *Smith (2019)*, *Smith and Jones (2019)*, *Smith et al. (2019)* (lowercase "et al." with the period after "al.").
- Reference list entries follow the target journal's style consistently.

Guide refs: §10 (Acknowledgments and References).

### Overall storytelling, process, and revision

What to look for:

- A reader can describe the paper's main contribution in one sentence.
- The five pivotal paragraphs (first paragraph of Introduction, last paragraph of Introduction, first paragraph of Results, first paragraph of Discussion, abstract) are noticeably more polished than surrounding text.
- Effort allocated where readers actually read: title, abstract, figures > intro/discussion > results > methods.
- The draft shows signs of outlining and iteration, not a single linear pass.

Guide refs: §11 (Process); §2 (all four principles); §3 (Anatomy of a paper).

### Writing style, concision, signposts

What to look for:

- Brevity as a revision task — cut 10–20% of the first draft. Remove hedges, nominalizations, metadiscourse, padding, and redundant phrases.
- Replace bloated phrases with plain alternatives (see the swap table in §12).
- Use signposts to mark key statements ("In this study, we tested whether…", "Overall, our experiments showed that…", "In conclusion, we found that…").
- Pronoun antecedents are unambiguous; the same noun is reused for the same concept.

Guide refs: §12 (Editing); §2 (Principles 2 and 4).

## Citation format in feedback

In the feedback `.txt`, each improvement bullet must end with a bracketed reference to the guide, naming the section number (and, where helpful, the named subsection):

```
- The introduction states the hypothesis only in the final paragraph; move it earlier and tie it to the gap defined in ¶1.  [ref: guide §6 (Introduction)]
```

Multiple guide sections can be separated by semicolons:

```
- Figure 2's title labels the axes but doesn't state the comparison; rewrite it to state the conclusion of the analysis.  [ref: guide §8 (Figures); guide §13 (Figures and tables checklist)]
```

Citation rules:

- The only citable source in feedback bullets is `comprehensive_guide_to_writing_a_scientific_paper.md`. Do not cite individual `lectures/` or `readings/` files.
- Every bullet must draw on a specific point from the named guide section. Bullets that name-drop a section without making a specific, criterion-tied observation should be rewritten or removed.
- Where the rubric maps multiple guide sections to a manuscript section, the feedback for that manuscript section should draw on more than one guide section across its bullets where the draft warrants it.
- The guide must have been opened with the Read tool in the current session before any bullet citing it is written. Citations are evidence the guide informed the bullet, not decoration.
